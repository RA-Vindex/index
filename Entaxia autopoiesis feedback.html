Entaxia, Autopoiesis, and Cyberpositive Feedback: Structural Propagation Beyond Self-Reproduction and Amplification

By Rodrigo Arellano-Voronov

November-December, 2012.

 

Abstract

This paper examines two dominant grammars through which systemic dynamics are commonly interpreted: autopoiesis and cyberpositive feedback. Autopoiesis explains how systems reproduce and maintain organizational identity through operational closure, while cyberpositive feedback accounts for recursive amplification within a given structural frame. Both frameworks illuminate important aspects of stability and instability across biological, social, and financial systems. Yet certain large-scale transformations—particularly regime shifts that alter structural architecture—resist full explanation within these terms.

To clarify this limitation, the paper introduces the concept of entaxia, defined as the propagation of structural constraints across metastable fields culminating in threshold events that reconfigure systemic topology. Entaxia is not proposed as a replacement for autopoiesis or feedback, but as a diagnostic lens for processes in which the frame of reproduction and amplification is itself transformed. By distinguishing identity-preserving self-production, variable amplification, and topological mutation, the paper develops an ontological account of systemic change grounded in constraint density, metastability, and immanence-breaking.

I. Introduction: Models of Systemic Dynamics and Their Limits

Contemporary analyses of complex systems—biological, financial, technological—draw upon a wide range of conceptual frameworks, including emergence theory, network topology, self-organization, evolutionary models, and systems sociology. Among these, two grammars have been especially influential in shaping how systemic dynamics are interpreted: autopoiesis and feedback. Autopoiesis, derived from biological systems theory and extended sociologically, emphasizes self-reproduction through operational closure. Feedback, particularly in its cyberpositive form, emphasizes recursive amplification of deviation within a given structural frame. Both have provided powerful explanatory tools across disciplines. Yet certain forms of systemic transformation—particularly those involving shifts in structural topology1 rather than merely pattern formation or escalation—remain insufficiently articulated within these dominant models.2

Autopoiesis, derived from the biological theory of Maturana and Varela and extended sociologically by Luhmann, emphasizes self-reproduction through operational closure. A system persists by recursively generating the elements that sustain its organization. Disturbances are processed internally; identity is maintained. Whether applied to living cells or financial communication networks, autopoiesis describes continuity under perturbation.

Feedback, particularly in its cyberpositive form, emphasizes recursive amplification of deviation within a system. Small fluctuations escalate through reinforcing loops. Prices rise and as they do, leverage expands because expansion reinforces expectation, instability then grows through recursive amplification. Feedback provides a powerful model of runaway dynamics without requiring external intervention.

These two grammars—self-reproduction and amplification—structure much of our understanding of systemic behavior. Yet, certain historical events, especially large-scale structural ruptures, resist being fully described with these terms. There are moments when systems do not merely reproduce themselves, nor simply amplify variables within a stable frame. Instead, the relational architecture itself shifts. Dependencies harden. Boundaries reconfigure. What previously functioned as an internal perturbation becomes a regime-altering transformation.

When such transformations occur, describing them either as autopoietic adaptation or as feedback escalation seems insufficient and incomplete. The difficulty is not that these frameworks are wrong, but that they may not be equipped to account for changes in topology—the reorganization of the structural field within which reproduction and amplification occur.

This paper therefore revisits the ontological assumptions underlying these models. It does not propose a corrective “solution” to autopoiesis or feedback, nor does it reject them outright. Rather, it isolates a distinct pattern of systemic change—one characterized by the propagation of structural constraints across metastable fields, culminating in threshold events that alter regime conditions.

For conceptual clarity, this pattern is termed entaxia.3

Entaxia does not replace autopoiesis or feedback. Systems may reproduce themselves autopoietically. They may amplify deviations through feedback. But there are instances in which constraint density escalates to the point that the frame of reproduction and amplification is itself transformed. It is this process—neither simple persistence nor mere acceleration—that the concept of entaxia seeks to make visible.

The distinction drawn in this paper is therefore not terminological but ontological. It concerns whether systemic change is understood as:

1. Identity-preserving self-production,
2. Variable amplification within a stable structure,
3. Or the mutation of the structure itself through escalating relational density.

Clarifying these differences is not an exercise in conceptual taxonomy. It is a necessary step toward understanding how complex systems transition from stability to rupture—not merely by moving faster or reproducing themselves more efficiently, but by intensifying their own constraint architecture beyond the limits of immanence.4

 


II. Autopoiesis: Self-Production and Operational Closure

The concept of autopoiesis was introduced by Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela (1972) to describe the distinctive organization of living systems. Rather than defining life by metabolism, reproduction, or adaptation alone, they proposed that a living system is characterized by its capacity to produce and maintain the components that constitute it as a unit. An autopoietic system is not merely self-regulating (homeostatic); it is self-producing (autopoietic). Its operations recursively generate the network of processes that define its identity. Autopoiesis thus shifts attention from substance to organization. A system is not alive because of what it is made of, but because of how its processes are organized. Organizational closure is central: the operations of the system refer back to and regenerate the system itself.

In biological terms, the paradigm case is the cell. A cell produces proteins and enzymes that sustain its membrane structure. The membrane, in turn, maintains the boundary conditions necessary for those biochemical processes to continue. This circularity is not tautological; it is generative. The system’s components continuously produce the network that produces them. Identity is preserved through recursive self-reference. Importantly, autopoiesis does not imply isolation. Living systems are structurally coupled to their environment. They exchange energy and matter. Yet they remain operationally closed: environmental perturbations do not directly determine the system’s internal operations; they trigger changes according to the system’s own structure. The system interprets disturbance through its own organization.

Niklas Luhmann (1984) extends this concept into social systems theory. For Luhmann, systems such as law, politics, science, and finance are not collections of individuals but networks of communication. They reproduce themselves through recursive communicative operations. The financial system, for example, reproduces itself through payments: each payment creates the conditions for further payments. Prices reference prior prices; expectations are formed through prior expectations; risk assessments recalibrate using historically internalized data. The system processes perturbations—market shocks, regulatory changes, crises—internally, translating them into its own code (e.g., payment/non-payment) while maintaining systemic identity.

Autopoiesis therefore explains continuity under disturbance. It accounts for how systems endure, adapt, and absorb shocks without dissolving. Perturbations may be severe, but as long as the organizational closure is preserved, the system remains what it is.

Crucially, autopoiesis presupposes:

• Organizational closure: operations are self-referential.
• Identity preservation: the system remains the same kind of system.
• Boundary maintenance: the distinction between system and environment persists.
• Perturbation processing without structural dissolution: crises are metabolized.

Even major disruptions, within this framework, are moments of adaptation within a stable organizational schema. The system may reorganize internally, but it does not cease to reproduce its defining operations.

However, autopoiesis is primarily a theory of persistence. It describes how systems maintain themselves across time. It is less equipped to explain structural mutation—moments when the organizational frame itself shifts, when boundary conditions are redrawn, or when the code that defines systemic operations is altered.

Autopoiesis can account for adaptation within a structure, but it does not inherently explain the transformation of that structure. It clarifies how systems endure, but it does not fully illuminate how they become something else.

 


III. Cyberpositive Feedback: Amplification Without Mutation

Cybernetics introduces a distinct explanatory grammar: feedback. Where autopoiesis emphasizes self-production and organizational closure, cybernetics focuses on recursive causality—how outputs of a system return as inputs, shaping subsequent behavior. Developed in the mid-twentieth century by figures such as Norbert Wiener (1948), W. Ross Ashby (1956), and later Jay Forrester (1961), cybernetics formalized the idea that systems can regulate or amplify themselves through feedback mechanisms.

Two basic forms of feedback were distinguished early on: Negative feedback stabilizes by counteracting deviation and maintaining equilibrium. A thermostat reduces heating when temperature rises above a set point; homeostatic biological systems regulate internal conditions through corrective responses. Wiener’s Cybernetics (1948) and Ashby’s work on homeostasis emphasized this regulatory dimension: systems remain viable through compensatory adjustment.

Positive feedback, by contrast, amplifies deviation. An initial increase in a variable produces further increases in that same variable. Rather than restoring balance, the loop reinforces movement away from an initial state. In engineering, such loops can produce runaway oscillations. In social and economic systems, they can generate explosive growth or collapse.

Classic examples include:

• Asset price bubbles: rising prices attract additional buyers, whose participation drives prices higher still.
• Margin spirals: falling asset prices trigger margin calls, forcing sales that push prices further downward.
• Network effects: adoption increases value, and increased value drives further adoption.

In each case, a variable—price, leverage, participation—enters a self-reinforcing cycle.

Cyberpositive feedback is analytically powerful because it explains nonlinear escalation without invoking external shocks. It accounts for exponential growth, cascading failures, contagion dynamics, and procyclical amplification. It has become central to financial theory, macroeconomic modeling, ecological systems analysis, and system dynamics.

Yet feedback theory typically presupposes a relatively stable structural frame within which amplification occurs. The recursive process modifies magnitudes—prices, volumes, exposure levels—but does not necessarily alter the architecture of relations that define the system.

Feedback modifies intensity. It accelerates processes already in motion. As deviation is recursively reinforced, the tempo of systemic change increases: prices rise more rapidly, leverage expands more aggressively, liquidity evaporates more abruptly. But it does not inherently reconfigure topology. A speculative bubble may inflate and burst while leaving the institutional structure of markets intact. A margin spiral may devastate balance sheets but preserve the rules of exchange, settlement, and collateralization. Even dramatic amplification can occur within a stable relational architecture.

Thus, while cyberpositive feedback explains escalation, overshoot, and instability, it does not automatically explain structural mutation. It describes how systems move faster, grow larger, or collapse more violently within a given frame. It does not, on its own, account for the transformation of that frame. This limitation becomes decisive when analyzing crises that alter institutional topology. If systemic rupture is interpreted solely as feedback escalation, attention remains focused on accelerating variables—leverage, prices, expectations—while overlooking shifts in constraint architecture. Feedback can destabilize a system. It does not necessarily explain how the system becomes structurally different.

Cybernetics offers a theory of recursive dynamics but it does not, by itself, provide a theory of topological transformation.

 


IV. Entaxia: Propagation of Structural Constraints

Entaxia designates a different order of systemic transformation. It does not describe how a system reproduces itself, nor how it accelerates through recursive amplification. It concerns the propagation of structural constraints across a metastable field—an escalation of relational density that eventually reconfigures the architecture within which reproduction and amplification occur.

Where autopoiesis preserves identity and feedback amplifies variables, entaxia alters the conditions under which identity and amplification are possible. Entaxia is not primarily concerned with: A. self-reproduction of components (autopoiesis); B. nor with recursive intensification of a parameter (feedback). Instead it is concerned with: C. the replication of relational constraints; D. the thickening of interdependencies; E. the escalation of topological density and F. the transformation of regime conditions.

The emphasis here is structural rather than kinetic. To understand entaxia, it is useful to turn to Simondon’s concept of metastability. A metastable system is neither in equilibrium nor in chaos; it contains unresolved tensions and latent potentials beneath an apparently stable surface. Stability in such systems is provisional. It masks a field of pre-individual tensions that can be actualized under specific conditions.

In metastable systems, change does not necessarily occur through external shock. It may arise from internal intensification. Latent incompatibilities accumulate. Dependencies multiply. Structural couplings thicken. The system appears coherent, but beneath the surface, relational density increases. Entaxia names this intensification of constraint architecture.

A structural constraint is not merely a rule or regulation; it is a relational dependency that limits possible configurations. Collateral requirements constrain leverage. Network centrality constrains flows. Clearing mechanisms constrain exposure patterns. As these constraints replicate across the field—through institutional imitation, regulatory harmonization, technological standardization—the density of relational coupling increases. Replication here does not mean reproduction of identical units. It means propagation of dependency patterns.

As constraint density escalates, the elasticity of the existing frame diminishes. The system becomes more sensitive to perturbations not because it moves faster, but because its internal architecture has thickened. What were once loosely coupled elements become tightly interlocked. Local disturbances propagate more easily across the network. When this density exceeds the capacity of the existing regime to absorb tension, immanence breaks.

Immanence refers to the operational horizon within which a system processes disturbance without altering its defining architecture. Under immanence, shocks are metabolized. Under entaxic escalation, shocks reveal structural limits. The field reorganizes. Topology mutates. This mutation is not merely a dramatic fluctuation. It is a reconfiguration of relational architecture: boundaries shift, hierarchies harden, nodes centralize, coupling patterns reorganize. The system that emerges may retain familiar components, but the relations among them have changed.

Entaxia therefore operates at the level of structural architecture: Not merely operational iteration, nor quantitative amplification, nor merely pattern emergence, but reconfiguration of the constraint field itself.

If autopoiesis explains how systems remain themselves, and feedback explains how systems intensify movement within themselves, entaxia explains how systems become structurally different. More importantly, entaxia does not imply teleology or design. It does not presuppose an external architect or an internal purpose. The propagation of constraints occurs immanently, through institutional practices, technical standards, regulatory codifications, and infrastructural layering. Human agency participates in these processes, but the resulting configuration is not reducible to intention. Constraint density escalates through distributed actions that collectively reconfigure the field.

Entaxia is thus neither organismic nor purely kinetic, but topological. It names the moment when structure ceases to merely support dynamics and becomes itself the site of transformation.

These models answer different analytical questions:

Autopoiesis: How does a system persist across time despite disturbance?
Feedback: How does deviation intensify within a system?
Entaxia: How does a system become structurally different?

Confusing these questions leads to analytical misclassification. Structural mutation may be misread as acceleration. Adaptive persistence may be mistaken for transformation. Amplification may be mistaken for architectural change. Distinguishing these logics does not invalidate any of them. Rather, it clarifies their scope. Systems may reproduce themselves autopoietically. They may amplify through feedback. And at certain thresholds, they may undergo entaxic reconfiguration.

The challenge is not to privilege one model universally, but to identify which logic is operative in a given historical or systemic moment.

 


VI. Why the Distinction Matters

The distinction among autopoiesis, feedback, and entaxia is not merely conceptual. It is diagnostic. Without distinguishing these processes, systemic crises are consistently misread.

If crises are interpreted as autopoietic disturbances, they appear as adaptive corrections within an otherwise stable system. Disruptions are metabolized. Regulatory adjustments, policy interventions, and market recalibrations are seen as evidence that the system remains fundamentally intact. The crisis becomes an episode of stress testing—a temporary deviation within a self-reproducing order. Such a metabolic interpretation of capitalism carries a particular danger: it implicitly anthropomorphizes the system as a living organism capable of digesting shocks, healing wounds, and restoring equilibrium. In doing so, it naturalizes crisis as a functional moment within systemic reproduction rather than as a possible site of structural rupture.

The language of metabolism suggests resilience and adaptation, obscuring the possibility that what appears as recovery may in fact be a reorganization of constraint architecture. When capitalism is framed as an organism that survives by processing disturbance, attention shifts away from shifts in topology—collateral hierarchies, clearing structures, funding dependencies—and toward the reassuring narrative of systemic endurance. The risk is not merely analytical error but political complacency: if crisis is digestion, transformation becomes improbable by definition.

Now, if crises are interpreted primarily through feedback dynamics, they appear as runaway amplification. Excess leverage, exuberant expectations, speculative manias, margin spirals—these become the explanatory center. Collapse is attributed to overshoot. The solution becomes dampening the loop: reduce leverage, increase buffers, slow acceleration.

Both interpretations capture real dynamics. Yet both risk overlooking a deeper transformation. When interpreted entaxically, crises are not merely disturbances or escalations. They are threshold events in structural density. They mark the moment when accumulated constraints—legal, infrastructural, financial, technological—have thickened to the point that the existing regime can no longer process perturbations without reorganizing itself. In this view, crisis reveals architecture.

A financial bubble may indeed involve feedback. Rising asset prices attract capital; leverage expands; optimism reinforces itself. But when the structure of collateralization shifts, when clearing mechanisms centralize risk, when liquidity hierarchies harden, the issue is no longer escalation of a variable. It is mutation of relational topology.

Just as acceleration is not sufficient for mutation, velocity is not equivalent to transformation. A system may move faster and remain the same kind of system. What transforms a regime is not speed, but density—the thickening of interdependencies that alters how perturbations propagate.

This distinction has analytical consequences. If crises are misread as feedback, policy will focus on smoothing cycles, reducing volatility, or moderating leverage. If crises are misread as autopoietic disturbances, it will be framed as an adaptive episode within systemic continuity. But if crisis is recognized as entaxic, the question shifts: what structural constraints have intensified to the point of architectural reconfiguration? What topological mutation has occurred?

In financial systems, this means examining: 1. Collateral hierarchies; 2. Clearing architectures; 3. Regulatory codifications; 4. Funding interdependencies; 5. Infrastructural centralization. These are not variables fluctuating within a frame; they are elements of the frame itself. The distinction therefore determines what becomes visible. Without it, structural mutation is mistaken for volatility. With it, volatility can be seen as a surface indicator of deeper architectural transformation.

Entaxic analysis does not deny feedback or adaptation. It situates them within a broader field in which the central question is no longer how systems move, but how their constraint architecture evolves. To mistake acceleration for mutation is to misdiagnose regime change. (see Open Letter on the Limits of Accelerationist Enthusiasm)

 


VII. Toward a Theory of Structural Intensification

Entaxia may be understood as a theory of structural intensification rather than acceleration. Systems do not necessarily mutate because they move faster. They mutate because relational density increases beyond their immanent capacity to absorb tension. Acceleration concerns velocity. Intensification concerns density.

A system may accelerate indefinitely within a stable architecture. Prices may fluctuate more rapidly. Communication may circulate more quickly. Transactions may settle at greater speed. Yet speed alone does not alter relational configuration. Acceleration compresses time; it does not automatically transform topology.

Structural intensification, by contrast, concerns the thickening of interdependencies. It occurs when constraints—legal, technical, financial, infrastructural—replicate across the field, narrowing the range of possible configurations. As coupling hardens, elasticity diminishes. Perturbations propagate more efficiently not because the system is faster, but because its architecture is denser.

In this sense, entaxia is not a kinetic theory but a topological one. It does not track trajectories of variables but patterns of constraint replication. Structural intensification alters how disturbance travels. It reorganizes the conditions under which feedback loops operate and autopoietic reproduction persists.

We can formulate thus: Autopoiesis describes continuity; feedback describes escalation; entaxia describes mutation.

These logics are not mutually exclusive. A system may reproduce itself autopoietically while amplifying deviations through feedback. But when constraint density intensifies beyond the elasticity of the regime, reproduction and amplification are subordinated to reconfiguration. The structural frame itself shifts. Recognizing this distinction allows us to reframe systemic crises not as mere accelerations or adaptive episodes, but as moments when relational architecture exceeds its immanent limits.

 


VIII. Conclusion: From Movement to Architecture

The central claim of this paper has been modest yet consequential: systemic transformation cannot be fully understood through the grammars of self-reproduction or recursive amplification alone. Autopoiesis explains how systems endure; feedback explains how they escalate. Neither, on its own, explains how the architecture of constraint mutates.

The conceptual risk of relying exclusively on organismic or kinetic metaphors is that structural change becomes either invisible or misclassified. If systems are imagined primarily as living organisms, crises appear as metabolic episodes within a self-preserving totality. If systems are imagined primarily as dynamic engines of acceleration, rupture appears as overshoot. In both cases, the possibility of topological transformation recedes from view.

Entaxia names a different analytic focus: the escalation of relational density within metastable fields. This approach directs attention away from variables and toward constraints, away from speed and toward architecture. Structural mutation does not necessarily manifest first in dramatic movement. It may begin as subtle thickening—legal codifications aligning across jurisdictions, collateral practices standardizing, infrastructural nodes centralizing, technical protocols synchronizing. These replications gradually alter the topology of the field until a threshold is crossed. At that threshold, crisis does not merely interrupt the system. It reveals it.

The task of systemic theory, then, is not to choose among autopoiesis, feedback, or entaxia as exclusive models, but to distinguish their domains of applicability. Persistence, escalation, and mutation are different processes. Confusing them produces analytical blindness.

To study complex systems responsibly requires shifting attention from movement to architecture, from velocity to density, from fluctuation to constraint. Only then can we discern whether a system is merely accelerating—or becoming structurally other. The future of systemic theory depends not on inventing new metaphors, but on recognizing when the old ones conceal more than they reveal.

1. The term topology is used here in its relational rather than geometric sense. In mathematics, topology studies properties of space preserved under continuous deformation, such as connectedness or continuity, rather than metric distance. In the present context, topology designates the configuration of relational constraints within a system—the pattern of interdependencies, couplings, and boundaries that determine how perturbations propagate. A topological mutation does not require new components; it involves a reorganization of how existing components are structurally related. The focus is therefore architectural rather than kinetic.

2. This paper does not attempt to survey the full landscape of complexity theory. Alternative frameworks include emergence theory in complexity science (Holland 1998; Kauffman 1993), far-from-equilibrium thermodynamics and dissipative structures (Prigogine 1984), network and graph theory (Barabási 2002; Watts 1999), assemblage theory (Deleuze & Guattari 1987; DeLanda 2006), actor-network theory (Latour 2005), evolutionary and adaptive systems models, and phase-transition approaches in statistical physics. Each offers valuable insights into systemic dynamics. The focus here is narrower: to clarify the distinction between identity-preserving self-reproduction, recursive amplification within stable architectures, and transformations involving shifts in structural constraint topology. The concept of entaxia is introduced within this limited analytic scope.

3. The term entaxia is derived from the Greek ἐν (en), meaning “in” or “within,” and τάξις (taxis), meaning “arrangement,” “ordering,” or “deployment.” Unlike syntaxis (ordering together) or taxis understood as external arrangement, entaxia emphasizes ordering from within—a structuring that emerges immanently through relational configuration rather than through external design. The term also resonates with biological and medical usages of ataxia (dis-order), suggesting that entaxia is not the imposition of harmony but the intensification of internal arrangement. In the present usage, entaxia does not denote teleological organization or equilibrium formation; rather, it designates the propagation of structural constraints within a field, whereby ordering intensifies until the topology of that field is transformed. The emphasis is therefore on immanent structuration rather than external governance or organismic self-maintenance.

4. The term immanence is used here in a structural rather than theological sense. Derived from the Latin immanere (“to remain within”), immanence designates a regime in which processes unfold according to internal relational conditions without reference to an external organizing principle. In Spinozist and later Deleuzian philosophy, immanence refers to a plane of consistency in which causes operate within the same ontological field as their effects. In the present usage, immanence denotes the operational horizon within which a system processes perturbations without altering its defining architecture. A system remains immanent so long as disturbances can be absorbed, redistributed, or metabolized without reconfiguring its structural constraints. Immanence-breaking, therefore, does not imply transcendence or external intervention; it refers to a threshold event in which internal constraint density exceeds the elasticity of the regime, leading to topological reorganization.
